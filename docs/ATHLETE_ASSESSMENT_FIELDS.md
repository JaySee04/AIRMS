# Athlete Assessment Report — Field Reference Guide

This document explains every column, score, and label found in the athlete assessment spreadsheet. It is intended for coaches, physiotherapists, analysts, or anyone interpreting the data for the first time.

---

## 1. Athlete Profile Fields

These are basic identification and biometric fields recorded at the time of assessment.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Full name of the athlete |
| **Age** | Age in years at time of assessment |
| **Sex** | Biological sex (used for physiological reference norms) |
| **Gender** | Self-identified gender |
| **Weight** | Body weight in kilograms (kg) |
| **Height** | Standing height in centimetres (cm) |
| **Sports** | Primary sport the athlete competes in |
| **Program** | Training program or development squad the athlete belongs to (e.g. PODIUM) |

---

## 2. Overall Performance Scores

These four scores summarise the athlete's general physical condition. **All scores are on a 0–100 scale, where higher is better.**

### Overall Physical Activity Score
A composite score aggregating mobility, stability, and symmetry into a single fitness index. Think of this as the athlete's general physical health grade.

- **80 and above** — Good overall condition
- **60–79** — Moderate; targeted improvement recommended
- **Below 60** — Significant intervention likely needed

### Injury Risk Index
A separate composite measuring the athlete's overall vulnerability to injury. **Unlike the scores above, lower is better here.**

- **0–10** — Low risk
- **11–20** — Moderate risk; monitoring advised
- **Above 20** — High risk; immediate attention recommended

---

## 3. Fitness Dimension Scores

Three sub-scores that feed into the Overall Physical Activity Score. Each is on the same **0–100 scale (higher = better)**.

### Mobility
Measures the athlete's range of motion across major joints — how freely they can move through a full range of movement patterns. Low mobility often precedes overuse injuries.

### Stability
Measures postural control and balance — the ability to maintain proper body position under load or during dynamic movement. Poor stability is a common contributor to joint injuries.

### Symmetry
Compares left-side vs. right-side performance across the body. A score closer to 100 means the two sides of the body are well balanced. Low symmetry often indicates a dominant side compensating for a weaker one, which increases long-term injury risk.

---

## 4. Exercise Risk Scores

These scores rate the injury risk at specific body regions. **Lower scores indicate lower risk.** There is no single fixed maximum — scores should be interpreted relative to each other and against population norms for the athlete's sport.

| Field | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Neck injury risk** | Vulnerability of the cervical spine and surrounding musculature |
| **Shoulder injury risk** | Risk at the shoulder joint, including rotator cuff and acromioclavicular structures |
| **Scoliosis** | Degree of lateral spinal curvature detected; elevated scores suggest referral for imaging |
| **Spinal disc herniation** | Risk of intervertebral disc stress or prolapse, particularly in the cervical and lumbar regions |
| **Lumbar-pelvis injury** | Risk across the lower back and pelvis complex — common in athletes with poor core stability or hip mobility |
| **Joint pain** | General joint discomfort score, aggregated across assessed joints |
| **Knee injury risk** | Risk at the knee joint, including ACL, meniscus, and patellofemoral structures |
| **Ankle injury risk** | Risk at the ankle joint, including ligament laxity and proprioceptive deficits |

> **Sport context matters.** A badminton player with elevated knee and ankle scores is expected given the demands of lateral movement and jump-landing. Risk scores should always be interpreted against sport-specific norms, not just absolute values.

## 5. Myodynamia Deficiency (Muscle Weakness)

This section flags muscles where the athlete shows **insufficient strength or activation** relative to expected norms for their sport and body weight.

The muscles assessed include:

- Biceps Brachii
- Pectoralis Major
- Lateral Deltoid
- Posterior Deltoid
- Rectus Abdominis
- External Oblique
- Internal Oblique
- Latissimus Dorsi
- Gluteus Maximus
- Gluteus Medius
- Piriformis
- Sartorius
- Vastus Lateralis
- Upper Trapezius
- Rectus Femoris
- Gluteus Minimus

### What the L / R / B Labels Mean

Each flagged muscle is assigned one of three laterality codes:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **L** | Deficiency detected on the **left side only** |
| **R** | Deficiency detected on the **right side only** |
| **B** | Deficiency detected on **both sides** |
| *(blank)* | No deficiency detected — muscle is within normal range |

A muscle appearing blank does not necessarily mean it is strong; it means it did not fall below the threshold for flagging.

---

## 6. Muscle Tension

This section flags muscles that are **excessively tight or overactivated** — the opposite problem to weakness. Chronic muscle tension restricts movement, alters biomechanics, and is a common precursor to strains and tears.

The muscles assessed include:

- Sternocleidomastoid
- Vastus Medialis
- Rectus Capitis Anterior
- Upper Trapezius
- Middle Deltoid
- Biceps Brachii
- Pectoralis Major
- External Oblique
- Internal Oblique
- Iliopsoas
- Gluteus Maximus
- Sartorius
- Vastus Medialis
- Biceps Femoris

The same **L / R / B** laterality coding applies here as in the Myodynamia Deficiency section.

---

## 7. Reading L / R / B in Context

The laterality codes become most meaningful when a muscle appears in **both** the weakness and tension sections. This pattern — weak on one side, tight overall — often signals a **compensation mechanism**: the body is overloading the tighter side to make up for the weaker one.

**Example from the data:**

The External Oblique is flagged as:
- **Weak on the left (L)** under Myodynamia Deficiency
- **Tight on both sides (B)** under Muscle Tension

This means the left oblique is not pulling its weight, so the right side is working harder to compensate — resulting in bilateral tightness. Left unaddressed, this asymmetry can contribute to elevated lumbar-pelvis and knee risk scores.

---

## 8. How to Use This Report

1. **Start with the overall scores** — they give a quick read on general fitness and injury vulnerability.
2. **Scan the exercise risk scores** — identify which body regions are the highest priority, especially in relation to the demands of the athlete's sport.
3. **Cross-reference the muscle flags** — look for muscles that appear in both weakness and tension sections. These are the most clinically significant findings.
4. **Note the laterality** — asymmetric flags (L or R only) suggest an imbalance that targeted unilateral training can address. Bilateral flags (B) may require more systemic intervention.
5. **Track changes over time** — a single snapshot is useful, but the real value of this data comes from comparing assessments across multiple time points to monitor progress or regression.

---

## 9. Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Myodynamia** | Muscle strength and force-generating capacity |
| **Deficiency** | A score falling below the expected threshold for the athlete's profile |
| **Laterality** | Which side of the body the finding applies to (left, right, or both) |
| **Compensation pattern** | When one muscle or body region overworks to cover for weakness elsewhere |
| **PODIUM** | A high-performance athlete development program |
| **Symmetry score** | A ratio comparing left and right side performance; 100 = perfect balance |
| **Exercise risk score** | A numerical estimate of injury likelihood at a specific anatomical region |
